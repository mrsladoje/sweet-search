\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

The agent, GCSN and sweep sets are held out from development but were \emph{previously examined} in an earlier version of this benchmark (Section~\ref{sec:workload}).
Only the BEIR sets are fresh held-out data.
Every table and figure says which kind it shows.
Ratios are rival / \sweet; above 1 means \sweet is faster.

\subsection{Warm calls}
\label{sec:warm}

Table~\ref{tab:warm} and Figure~\ref{fig:ratios} give the main comparison.
Each rival is at the faster of its two frozen configurations, so ``all cores'' is not always the tested condition: on the M3 Max, several rivals are fastest with one thread.

On agent and GCSN calls (previously examined), \sweet was faster than every exact rival with float32 inputs on all five CPUs.
The fastest such rival needed \nm{best.warm.agent-ho.f32.min}--\nm{best.warm.agent-ho.f32.max}$\times$ the time on agent calls and \nm{best.warm.gcsn-ho.f32.min}--\nm{best.warm.gcsn-ho.f32.max}$\times$ on GCSN calls.
On BEIR calls (fresh), \sweet was faster on AMD, Graviton4, the M3 Max and the EPYC; the closest float32 rival needed \nm{best.warm.beir.f32.nointel.min}--\nm{best.warm.beir.f32.nointel.max}$\times$ the time.
On Intel BEIR calls, PyTorch with float32 inputs (padded flat layout) was faster: it needed \nm{warm.intel.beir.torch.f32}$\times$ the time of \sweet.
The frozen primary PyTorch configuration of that family was slower than \sweet; the faster one is the second frozen configuration.
BEIR calls have 32 query tokens and up to 200 candidates of about 166 tokens, so they are larger than the agent and GCSN calls.
The pooled BEIR row can hide losses on single sets. Set by set, PyTorch with bfloat16 inputs was faster on \nm{beirset.arm.PyTorch.bf16} of the 20 sets on Graviton4 and \nm{beirset.intel.PyTorch.bf16} on Intel, PyTorch with float32 inputs on \nm{beirset.m3max.PyTorch.f32} sets on the M3 Max, and NumPy on \nm{beirset.epyc.NumPy.f32} set on the EPYC.

Two more rival types were faster in some cells.
NumKong, which approximates the maximum (Section~\ref{sec:contracts}), was faster on Intel and Graviton4 on all three sets, and slightly faster on M3 Max BEIR calls (\nm{warm.m3max.beir.numkong.best}$\times$). On AMD it was faster on \nm{beirset.amd.NumKong.f32} of the 20 BEIR sets, although not on the pooled row.
PyTorch with bfloat16 inputs, which can use the AMX tile unit on the Xeon, was faster on Intel BEIR calls (\nm{warm.intel.beir.torch.bf16}$\times$).
Its inputs are rounded to bfloat16, which changes scores by up to about $2\times10^{-3}$ (Table~\ref{tab:contract-effects}); its own arithmetic adds an error of similar size (Table~\ref{tab:correctness}).

The product path (Node.js, N-API, the same kernel) took \nm{range.warm.node-production.min}--\nm{range.warm.node-production.max}$\times$ the time of the bare kernel.
The previous production path of the engine (one JavaScript object per candidate into an older kernel) was \nm{range.warm.node-legacy.min}--\nm{range.warm.node-legacy.max}$\times$ slower than the bare kernel.
When the native addon is missing, the engine falls back to WebAssembly and then to plain JavaScript.
On agent calls these tiers were \nm{range.tiers.agent-ho.node-wasm.min}--\nm{range.tiers.agent-ho.node-wasm.max}$\times$ and \nm{range.tiers.agent-ho.node-js.min}--\nm{range.tiers.agent-ho.node-js.max}$\times$ slower than the native product path.

\subsection{Quality on BEIR: int4 against float32}
\label{sec:quality}

\paragraph{The pre-registered test.}
Before the freeze we fixed four primary comparisons (both models $\times$ both datasets, $k = 100$), the margin $\delta = 0.01$ absolute nDCG@10, and the decision rule.
For each set, ``no material loss'' holds only if two conditions hold: the lower bound of the paired-bootstrap 95\% interval of (K $-$ F) is above $-\delta$, and the Holm-adjusted one-sided $p$ for $H_0$: loss $\geq \delta$ is below 0.05.
K is the kernel's own recorded scores.
F scores the float32 vectors in float64 without clamp.
The margin was set by the authors before the freeze; it is 1.6\% of the SciFact and 3.1\% of the NFCorpus baseline for ColBERTv2.
We ran the test on every CPU, but the CPUs are not independent replications: they share the queries, and the kernel's rankings are almost the same on all of them (see below).
Table~\ref{tab:primary} gives the result; $p = 0.0004$ is the smallest value that 10,000 resamples and the Holm factor 4 can give.
The interval condition acts at a one-sided level of 0.025 per set and the Holm condition at a family level of 0.05; we required both so that neither a wide interval nor a single small $p$ decides alone.
The verdicts do not depend on the exact margin: ColBERTv2 passes the interval condition for any $\delta$ above 0.0021, and answerai-colbert-small fails it for any $\delta$ below 0.0497.

For ColBERTv2, both sets pass: K $-$ F is \nm{q.intel.scifact.colbertv2.diff} on SciFact and \nm{q.intel.nfcorpus.colbertv2.diff} on NFCorpus.
For answerai-colbert-small, both sets fail: K $-$ F is \nm{q.intel.scifact.answersmall.diff} (\nm{q.intel.scifact.answersmall.rel}\%) on SciFact and \nm{q.intel.nfcorpus.answersmall.diff} (\nm{q.intel.nfcorpus.answersmall.rel}\%) on NFCorpus.
On NFCorpus this reranking is worse than the BM25 order itself (\nm{nd.nfcorpus.answersmall.K} against \nm{nd.nfcorpus.answersmall.bm25}).
On SciFact, even float32 ColBERTv2 reranking (F, \nm{nd.scifact.colbertv2.F}) did not reach the BM25 order (\nm{nd.scifact.colbertv2.bm25}); the quality tests compare storage formats, not reranking against no reranking.
The ColBERTv2 paper reports 0.693 (SciFact) and 0.338 (NFCorpus) for end-to-end retrieval~\cite{santhanam2022colbertv2}; our values are lower, as expected when reranking only BM25's top 100 with PyLate's default 180-token documents.
K and Q+c (int4 decoded in float64, contract S) give the same nDCG@10 to four decimals in every cell, on every CPU and $k$.

\paragraph{Where the loss comes from (post hoc).}
Table~\ref{tab:effects} separates the score sources at $k = 100$.
The clamp changes no nDCG@10 value (F+c = F), and the kernel's floating-point arithmetic changes none at four decimals (K = Q+c).
The loss enters between F+c and Q+c: with the int4 storage under contract S.
The stored norms are 1 for both models, because the models return unit-length token vectors.
Contract S therefore divides by 1 and keeps the length of the decoded token.
That length differs from 1 by a similar amount for both models: the middle 80\% of decoded tokens lie between \nm{int4diag.scifact.colbertv2.lenp10} and \nm{int4diag.scifact.colbertv2.lenp90} (ColBERTv2) and between \nm{int4diag.scifact.answersmall.lenp10} and \nm{int4diag.scifact.answersmall.lenp90} (answerai-colbert-small).
The models differ in their score margins.
For answerai-colbert-small, the candidates of a query differ little: the median gap between the first and the tenth score is \nm{margin.scifact.answersmall}\% (SciFact) and \nm{margin.nfcorpus.answersmall}\% (NFCorpus) of the first score.
For ColBERTv2 it is \nm{margin.scifact.colbertv2}\% and \nm{margin.nfcorpus.colbertv2}\%.
A possible explanation is that a length error of about 1\% can reorder answerai-colbert-small's candidates more often than ColBERTv2's; the first-to-tenth gap is a coarse measure, so this is not a demonstrated mechanism.
The direction error of a decoded token (cosine about 0.995) affects contracts S and C alike; the length error affects only S, which fits the observation that C removes most of the loss.
Contract C divides by the length of the decoded token; on the same int4 data it gives \nm{nd.scifact.answersmall.Cb} on SciFact and \nm{nd.nfcorpus.answersmall.Cb} on NFCorpus, against float32 values of \nm{nd.scifact.answersmall.F} and \nm{nd.nfcorpus.answersmall.F}.
It removes most, not all, of the loss, and it would still fail the margin of 0.01 on both sets: its own difference to F is \nm{ph.scifact.answersmall.Cb.diff} [\nm{ph.scifact.answersmall.Cb.lo}, \nm{ph.scifact.answersmall.Cb.hi}] and \nm{ph.nfcorpus.answersmall.Cb.diff} [\nm{ph.nfcorpus.answersmall.Cb.lo}, \nm{ph.nfcorpus.answersmall.Cb.hi}].
This analysis is post hoc and descriptive.
We found the loss on the fresh held-out data, so any changed scoring rule needs new data for its test.
The loss belongs to the scoring contract that \sweet implements, not to its floating-point arithmetic.

\paragraph{NumKong's quality.}
NumKong's own scores fell far below the exact value of its own contract with answerai-colbert-small: \nm{nd.scifact.answersmall.NK} against \nm{nd.scifact.answersmall.Ca} on SciFact, below even the BM25 order.
With ColBERTv2 the loss was small (\nm{nd.scifact.colbertv2.NK} against \nm{nd.scifact.colbertv2.Ca}).
On BEIR calls its top-ranked document agreed with the contract-C reference of its own inputs on only \nm{nk.top1own.beir.min}--\nm{nk.top1own.beir.max}\% of calls (agent calls: \nm{nk.top1own.agent-ho.min}--\nm{nk.top1own.agent-ho.max}\%), so the approximation, not the contract, causes most of the disagreement; its bfloat16 path on the Xeon, which uses AMX and returned no NaN, agreed with contract S on 78\%.
A post-hoc check on synthetic vectors explains the collapse (\texttt{numkong\_check.py}).
Our Rust loop and NumKong's own Python interface returned identical scores (largest difference \nm{nkcheck.adapter}), so the adapter is not the cause.
When the candidates' scores lay about \nm{nkcheck.random.gap}\% apart, NumKong's candidate order kept a rank correlation of \nm{nkcheck.random.rho.min}--\nm{nkcheck.random.rho.max} with the exact order.
When they lay about \nm{nkcheck.clustered.gap}\% apart, the correlation fell to \nm{nkcheck.clustered.rho.min}--\nm{nkcheck.clustered.rho.max}, and the top document agreed in \nm{nkcheck.clustered.top1.min}--\nm{nkcheck.clustered.top1.max}\% of queries.
Dimension 96 and 128 behaved the same.
So NumKong's one-token refinement fails when candidate scores lie close together, as they do for answerai-colbert-small, and holds up better when they lie far apart, as for ColBERTv2.

\paragraph{The kernel across CPUs.}
The kernel's BEIR scores were bit-identical across the three x86 CPUs (AVX-512 on Intel and AMD, AVX2 on the EPYC, by design) and between Graviton4 and the M3 Max (both NEON).
Between x86 and ARM, \nm{xplat.armequal.lo}--\nm{xplat.armequal.hi}\% of scores were bit-identical, and the largest difference was \nm{xplat.maxabs}.
The full candidate ranking was the same on at least \nm{xplat.sameranking.min}\% of calls, and nDCG@10 agreed to four decimals.
Within one CPU, the scores of all thread budgets, rounds and processes were bit-identical (\nm{xplat.files} result files, \nm{xplat.scores} million scores).

\subsection{Quality per latency budget}
\label{sec:budget}

A faster scorer can rerank more candidates in the same time.
Figure~\ref{fig:budget} plots nDCG@10 against the warm replay p95 of the timed scoring call (the 95th percentile over all timed repetitions of the BEIR calls, not a service-request distribution), one point per $k$. Each point's quality comes from the arm's first run; every rival's scores were bit-identical across its runs, except NumKong's Python arms on Intel.
Table~\ref{tab:budget} reads it at two budgets.
The time covers only the scoring call: the rivals' decoding and casting run outside their timer.
The dashed line is a constructed estimate for float32 storage: the reference quality F paired with the time of the fastest frozen exact float32 rival on the same shapes. No single run produced these pairs; the estimate assumes that the rival's time does not depend on the vector values and that its scores would match F. The dotted line is the BM25 order without reranking.

At a p95 budget of 0.25\,ms, \sweet reached a larger $k$ than every exact rival on AMD.
On Intel the best exact rival (PyTorch with bfloat16 inputs) reached a larger $k$ at both budgets.
On Graviton4, the M3 Max and the EPYC, \sweet and the best exact rival reached the same $k$, or the rival reached a larger one.
For ColBERTv2, more candidates do not always help: on SciFact, nDCG@10 is highest at $k = 25$.
For answerai-colbert-small, the constructed float32-storage estimate lies above every int4 arm at the same budget on every CPU, because of the loss above.
NumKong reached $k = 200$ within 0.25\,ms on the three cloud CPUs.
On Intel its bfloat16 path, which uses AMX, also gave better quality than \sweet with answerai-colbert-small at a lower p95 (Table~\ref{tab:budget}); on the other CPUs NumKong's quality with that model collapsed.
The budget view reads the largest $k$ that fits, which is not always the $k$ with the best quality.

\subsection{Pool-parked calls (5\,ms busy gap)}
\label{sec:parked}

When other work runs for 5\,ms before each call, the worker pool of \sweet has parked and must wake.
Table~\ref{tab:parked} shows that this changes the ranking.
NumPy was faster than \sweet on the EPYC (\nm{parked.epyc.agent-ho.numpy.f32}$\times$ and \nm{parked.epyc.gcsn-ho.numpy.f32}$\times$), on Intel (\nm{parked.intel.agent-ho.numpy.f32}$\times$ on agent calls, \nm{parked.intel.gcsn-ho.numpy.f32}$\times$ on GCSN calls, \nm{parked.intel.beir.numpy.f32}$\times$ on BEIR calls), on Graviton4 (\nm{parked.arm.agent-ho.numpy.f32}$\times$ and \nm{parked.arm.gcsn-ho.numpy.f32}$\times$) and on the M3 Max (\nm{parked.m3max.agent-ho.numpy.f32}$\times$ and \nm{parked.m3max.gcsn-ho.numpy.f32}$\times$).
On AMD, \sweet stayed faster than every exact rival.
The product path calls a prewarm function when the rerank stage starts, so the pool wakes while other work runs.
In this mode the harness runs 0.1\,ms of busy work between the prewarm and the call, and that time is outside the timer.
With it, a call took \nm{range.parked.prewarm.agent.min}--\nm{range.parked.prewarm.agent.max}$\times$ the time of the bare kernel on agent calls.
The result depends on that overlap, and the rivals had no prewarm, so this comparison is not symmetric.
With prewarm, \sweet was still slower than NumPy on Intel GCSN calls (\nm{parked.intel.gcsn-ho.node-prewarm} against \nm{parked.intel.gcsn-ho.numpy.f32}) and on Graviton4 GCSN calls (\nm{parked.arm.gcsn-ho.node-prewarm} against \nm{parked.arm.gcsn-ho.numpy.f32}).
On the Mac and the EPYC box the BEIR busy-gap groups were not run (Appendix~\ref{app:deviations}).

\subsection{Parallel callers}
\label{sec:conc}

In a service, several requests can score at the same time.
We ran $N \in \{1, 4, 16\}$ callers in two executors.
The \emph{threads} executor runs $N$ threads in one process, so the library's pool is shared, as in one service process.
The \emph{procs} executor runs one process per caller with a thread budget of $\max(1, \text{cores}/N)$; a configuration with a fixed thread count keeps that count, which oversubscribed the CPU in \nm{conc.oversub.n} rival runs (marked $\ddagger$).
On the EPYC, the best exact rival at 16 callers was such an oversubscribed run (marked in Table~\ref{tab:conc}); on the other CPUs it was not. Figure~\ref{fig:conc} includes the oversubscribed runs.
\emph{Closed-loop saturation} lets each caller replay its share of the set back to back; we report throughput.
\emph{Open-loop} runs use one Poisson arrival trace per rate (seed 42; the same timestamps and call order for every arm).
The rates are absolute and common to all arms on a machine.
They were frozen as 25, 50 and 75\% of the closed-loop throughput of the slowest shortlisted rival on development data, but that throughput came from the uncorrected per-process wall time (below), so we treat them as fixed stress rates, not as utilization levels.
Their latency includes queueing.
The threads executor feeds one shared queue; the procs executor assigns each request to one process in advance, and its arrival loops spin on the same cores that score.
Each configuration ran once, for 20 to 500\,ms, so these results have no uncertainty interval.
The harness does not check the scores of these runs, and the AMD BLIS arms are left out: the parallel-caller harness ignored their environment and ran plain NumPy.

Table~\ref{tab:conc} and Figure~\ref{fig:conc} give the results.
With one caller, \sweet had \nm{range.conc.n1.min}--\nm{range.conc.n1.max}$\times$ the throughput of the best exact rival in the same executor (threads).
With 16 callers the result depends on the executor.
With one process per caller, an exact rival had the higher throughput in \nm{conc.n16.procs.lost} of 10 CPU--workload pairs.
In one shared process, an exact rival had the higher throughput in \nm{conc.n16.threads.lost} of 10 pairs, and the throughput of \sweet fell as callers were added.
We did not test why; possible causes are the shared worker pool, the Python interpreter lock around each call, and the different warm-up of the two executors. The product runs in Node.js, which has no such lock.
NumKong had a higher throughput than \sweet at 16 callers in most pairs.
At the highest common open-loop rate, the best exact rival had a lower per-request p99 than \sweet on M3 Max GCSN calls only.
On M3 Max agent calls neither arm kept up with that rate ($\S$: \sweet achieved \nm{conc.m3max.agent.open.achieved.ours} calls/s), so both p99 values there measure queue growth, not a steady state.

During the analysis we found a defect in the procs executor: in closed-loop runs the callers start about 50\,ms before the recorded start time, so the recorded wall time is too short.
We corrected these throughputs by adding 50\,ms, the time between the start file and the recorded start. No caller can start before the start file exists, so the corrected wall time is an upper bound and every corrected throughput is a lower bound; a ratio of two such bounds has no bound.
For \sweet the correction is large: it makes up at least \nm{conc.corr.share.min}\% of the corrected time, and in \nm{conc.corr.negative} of \nm{conc.corr.runs} runs the recorded wall time was negative.
A check supports the correction: with one caller, where the executor should matter little, corrected procs and threads throughputs of \sweet agree within 2.5\% on the six cloud CPU--workload pairs; on the M3 Max they differ by up to 1.9$\times$, for reasons we did not find.
Per-request latencies need no correction (Appendix~\ref{app:deviations}).

\subsection{Memory}
\label{sec:memory}

A separate, untimed pass per arm and set measured resident memory in a fresh process.
On Linux it reads \texttt{VmRSS}, \texttt{VmHWM} and \texttt{smaps\_rollup}; on macOS a 0.2\,ms sampler reads the resident size.
The allocator returned freed large blocks at once, so a later phase cannot reuse them unseen.
Table~\ref{tab:memory} reports resident bytes per stored document token and the peak temporary memory of the largest call.
The first number adds the computed size of the int4 store (for arms that read it) to the measured resident size of the arm's own prepared copy.
The memory pass uses other allocator settings than the timing runs, and the Mac's 0.2\,ms sampler can miss short peaks, so Mac peaks are lower bounds.
\sweet holds \nm{mem.intel.ours.bpt} bytes per token at dimension 128 and \nm{mem.intel.ours.bpt96} at dimension 96, the size of the int4 store itself; this follows from the format.
The float32 rivals hold their decoded copy: about 520 bytes per token for NumPy and \nm{mem.intel.torch_pad_bmm.bpt} for padded PyTorch on Intel.
NumKong's float32 arms hold more than 512 bytes per token; we did not analyse why.
The int4-self rivals hold the store and peak at about 280\,MB of temporary memory on the largest agent call.

\subsection{Instance-to-instance spread}
\label{sec:spread}

A replica instance of each cloud type repeated the agent and GCSN sets.
Across \nm{replica.n} rival ratios, the ratio on the replica differed from the first instance by a median of \nm{replica.median}\% and at most \nm{replica.maxabs}\%.
No ratio changed sides of 1 (Table~\ref{tab:replica}).
The kernel's own geometric-mean latency differed by at most \nm{replica.oursmax}\%.
Each replica was a different instance in the same availability zone.
EC2 does not expose the physical host of shared-tenancy instances, so we cannot show that the hosts differed.
The BEIR and sweep sets have no replica; differences smaller than about 13\% on them, such as AMD BEIR NumKong (1.06) or M3 Max BEIR NumKong (0.99), may not repeat on another instance.
