\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

The agent, GCSN and sweep sets are held out from development, but we had seen aggregate results on them before the freeze (\emph{previously examined}, Section~\ref{sec:workload}).
Only the BEIR sets are fresh held-out data.
Every table and figure says which kind it shows.
Ratios are rival / \sweet; above 1 means \sweet is faster.
Most results follow the frozen protocol.
Headings, captions and paragraphs marked ``post-freeze'' or ``post hoc'' report checks that we added after the freeze; these checks change no frozen test result.

\subsection{Warm calls}
\label{sec:warm}

On the captured agent and GCSN calls, \sweet was faster than every exact float32 rival on all five CPUs; on the fresh BEIR calls, PyTorch was faster on Intel.
Table~\ref{tab:warm} and Figure~\ref{fig:ratios} give the main comparison.
Each rival is at the faster of its two frozen configurations, so ``all cores'' is not always the tested condition: on the M3 Max, several rivals are fastest with one thread.

On agent and GCSN calls (previously examined), the fastest exact float32 rival needed \nm{best.warm.agent-ho.f32.min}--\nm{best.warm.agent-ho.f32.max}$\times$ the time on agent calls and \nm{best.warm.gcsn-ho.f32.min}--\nm{best.warm.gcsn-ho.f32.max}$\times$ on GCSN calls.
On BEIR calls (fresh), \sweet was faster on AMD, Graviton4, the M3 Max and the EPYC; the closest float32 rival needed \nm{best.warm.beir.f32.nointel.min}--\nm{best.warm.beir.f32.nointel.max}$\times$ the time.
On Intel BEIR calls, PyTorch with float32 inputs (padded flat layout) was faster: it needed \nm{warm.intel.beir.torch.f32}$\times$ the time of \sweet.
The frozen primary PyTorch configuration of that family was slower than \sweet; the faster one is the second frozen configuration.
BEIR calls have 32 query tokens and up to 200 candidates of about 166 tokens, so they are larger than the agent and GCSN calls.
The pooled BEIR row can hide losses on single sets. Set by set, PyTorch with bfloat16 inputs was faster on \nm{beirset.arm.PyTorch.bf16} of the 20 sets on Graviton4 and \nm{beirset.intel.PyTorch.bf16} on Intel, PyTorch with float32 inputs on \nm{beirset.m3max.PyTorch.f32} sets on the M3 Max, and NumPy on \nm{beirset.epyc.NumPy.f32} set on the EPYC.

Two more rival types were faster in some cells.
NumKong, which approximates the maximum (Section~\ref{sec:contracts}), was faster on Intel and Graviton4 on all three sets. On M3 Max BEIR calls its ratio was \nm{warm.m3max.beir.numkong.best}$\times$, which does not meet our rule for ``faster''. On AMD it was faster on \nm{beirset.amd.NumKong.f32} of the 20 BEIR sets, although not on the pooled row.
PyTorch with bfloat16 inputs, which can use the AMX tile unit on the Xeon, was faster on Intel BEIR calls (\nm{warm.intel.beir.torch.bf16}$\times$).
Its inputs are rounded to bfloat16, which changes scores by up to about $2\times10^{-3}$ (Table~\ref{tab:contract-effects}); its own arithmetic adds an error of similar size (Table~\ref{tab:correctness}).

The product path (Node.js, N-API, the same kernel) took \nm{range.warm.node-production.min}--\nm{range.warm.node-production.max}$\times$ the time of the bare kernel.
The previous production path of the engine (one JavaScript object per candidate into an older kernel) was \nm{range.warm.node-legacy.min}--\nm{range.warm.node-legacy.max}$\times$ slower than the bare kernel.
When the native addon is missing, the engine falls back to WebAssembly and then to plain JavaScript.
On agent calls these tiers were \nm{range.tiers.agent-ho.node-wasm.min}--\nm{range.tiers.agent-ho.node-wasm.max}$\times$ and \nm{range.tiers.agent-ho.node-js.min}--\nm{range.tiers.agent-ho.node-js.max}$\times$ slower than the native product path.

\subsection{Size sweep}
\label{sec:sweep}

\sweet did not win at every call size: its losses form bands of call shapes, and the bands differ by CPU.
Figure~\ref{fig:sweep} shows, per sweep cell, the fastest frozen exact rival with float32 inputs against \sweet.
On the cloud CPUs all 84 cells ran.
The M3 Max ran \nm{sweep.m3max.cells} cells and the EPYC \nm{sweep.epyc.cells}: a reduced grid of 24, decided before any result, plus 2 and 4 complete cells from before that decision.
The fastest exact float32 rival had a ratio below 1 in \nm{sweep.intel.lose} of \nm{sweep.intel.cells} cells on Intel, \nm{sweep.amd.lose} on AMD, \nm{sweep.arm.lose} on Graviton4, \nm{sweep.m3max.lose} of \nm{sweep.m3max.cells} on the M3 Max and \nm{sweep.epyc.lose} of \nm{sweep.epyc.cells} on the EPYC.
If bfloat16 rivals count too, the counts are \nm{sweep.intel.loseany}, \nm{sweep.amd.loseany}, \nm{sweep.arm.loseany}, \nm{sweep.m3max.loseany} and \nm{sweep.epyc.loseany}.
The worst cell was on Intel: PyTorch with bfloat16 inputs needed \nm{sweep.intel.worstany}$\times$ the time of \sweet (\nm{sweep.intel.worstany.where}).
With float32 inputs the worst cell needed \nm{sweep.intel.worst}$\times$ (Intel, \nm{sweep.intel.worstcell}).

On Intel, every loss is to PyTorch: 33 of the 56 cells with 32 or 64 query tokens, at pool sizes from 1 to 4,096 documents, and 4 cells with 8 query tokens.
On AMD, PyTorch was faster for pools of 64 to 1,024 short documents at every query length, while \sweet was faster for smaller and larger pools.
On Graviton4, the losses are at 32 and 64 query tokens, mostly with 256 to 4,096 documents (PyTorch) and with single documents (NumPy).
On the M3 Max, maxsim-cpu and PyTorch (both on Apple Accelerate) were faster for single documents with 32 or 64 query tokens and for the largest pools.
At 8 query tokens, \sweet was faster in every cell on Graviton4 and the M3 Max.
We did not decompose the cost of a call (Section~\ref{sec:limits}), so these figures show where \sweet loses, not why.
NumKong is not in the figure; it was faster than \sweet in \nm{nksweep.intel} (Intel), \nm{nksweep.amd} (AMD), \nm{nksweep.arm} (Graviton4) and \nm{nksweep.m3max} (M3 Max) cells.

\subsection{Quality on BEIR: int4 against float32}
\label{sec:quality}

The int4 scoring rule passed the pre-registered test for ColBERTv2 and failed it for answerai-colbert-small.
The loss comes from the scoring contract, not from the kernel's arithmetic.

\paragraph{The pre-registered test.}
Before the freeze we fixed four primary comparisons (both models $\times$ both datasets, $k = 100$), the margin $\delta = 0.01$ absolute nDCG@10, and the decision rule.
For each set, ``no material loss'' holds only if two conditions hold: the lower bound of the paired-bootstrap 95\% interval of (K $-$ F) is above $-\delta$, and the Holm-adjusted one-sided $p$ for $H_0$: loss $\geq \delta$ is below 0.05.
K is the kernel's own recorded scores.
F scores the float32 vectors in float64 without clamp.
The margin was set by the authors before the freeze; it is 1.6\% of the SciFact and 3.1\% of the NFCorpus baseline for ColBERTv2.
We ran the test on every CPU, but the CPUs are not independent replications: they share the queries, and the kernel's rankings are almost the same on all of them (see below).
Table~\ref{tab:primary} gives the result; $p = 0.0004$ is the smallest value that 10,000 resamples and the Holm factor 4 can give.
The interval condition acts at a one-sided level of 0.025 per set and the Holm condition at a family level of 0.05; we required both so that neither a wide interval nor a single small $p$ decides alone.
The verdicts do not depend on the exact margin: ColBERTv2 passes the interval condition for any $\delta$ above 0.0021, and answerai-colbert-small fails it for any $\delta$ below 0.0497.

For ColBERTv2, both sets pass: K $-$ F is \nm{q.intel.scifact.colbertv2.diff} on SciFact and \nm{q.intel.nfcorpus.colbertv2.diff} on NFCorpus.
For answerai-colbert-small, both sets fail: K $-$ F is \nm{q.intel.scifact.answersmall.diff} (\nm{q.intel.scifact.answersmall.rel}\%) on SciFact and \nm{q.intel.nfcorpus.answersmall.diff} (\nm{q.intel.nfcorpus.answersmall.rel}\%) on NFCorpus.
On NFCorpus this reranking is worse than the BM25 order itself (\nm{nd.nfcorpus.answersmall.K} against \nm{nd.nfcorpus.answersmall.bm25}).
On SciFact, even float32 ColBERTv2 reranking (F, \nm{nd.scifact.colbertv2.F}) did not reach the BM25 order (\nm{nd.scifact.colbertv2.bm25}).
The ColBERTv2 paper reports 0.693 (SciFact) and 0.338 (NFCorpus) for end-to-end retrieval~\cite{santhanam2022colbertv2}.
A likely cause of our lower values is document length.
We encoded documents with each model's default length: 180 tokens for ColBERTv2 and 300 for answerai-colbert-small.
The two models use the same tokenizer, and the 180-token limit cut \nm{beir.cut.scifact}\% of SciFact and \nm{beir.cut.nfcorpus}\% of NFCorpus documents.
A check after the freeze supports this cause (Appendix~\ref{app:deviations}).
We encoded the same documents again with the same model revision, at 180 and at 300 tokens.
At 180 tokens, F was \nm{c300.scifact.180} on SciFact and \nm{c300.nfcorpus.180} on NFCorpus, the same as in the main run.
At 300 tokens, F rose to \nm{c300.scifact.300} and \nm{c300.nfcorpus.300}, close to the published values and above the BM25 order on both sets.
The int4 result for ColBERTv2 also holds at 300 tokens.
In the same check, we stored the 300-token documents in int4 and scored them with the kernel (K) on the M3 Max.
K $-$ F was \nm{i300.scifact.300.KF} [\nm{i300.scifact.300.KF.lo}, \nm{i300.scifact.300.KF.hi}] on SciFact and \nm{i300.nfcorpus.300.KF} [\nm{i300.nfcorpus.300.KF.lo}, \nm{i300.nfcorpus.300.KF.hi}] on NFCorpus, inside the margin of 0.01 on both sets.
At 180 tokens, the same check gave \nm{i300.scifact.180.KF} and \nm{i300.nfcorpus.180.KF}, the same as the main run.
This check is post hoc and reuses the BEIR queries of the main test, so it is descriptive, not a new confirmatory test.
K and Q+c (int4 decoded in float64, contract S) give the same nDCG@10 to four decimals in every cell, on every CPU and $k$.

\paragraph{Where the loss comes from (post hoc).}
Table~\ref{tab:effects} separates the score sources at $k = 100$.
The clamp changes no nDCG@10 value (F+c = F), and the kernel's floating-point arithmetic changes none at four decimals (K = Q+c).
The loss enters between F+c and Q+c: with the int4 storage under contract S.
The stored norms are 1 for both models, because the models return unit-length token vectors.
Contract S therefore divides by 1 and keeps the length of the decoded token.
That length differs from 1 by a similar amount for both models: the middle 80\% of decoded tokens lie between \nm{int4diag.scifact.colbertv2.lenp10} and \nm{int4diag.scifact.colbertv2.lenp90} (ColBERTv2) and between \nm{int4diag.scifact.answersmall.lenp10} and \nm{int4diag.scifact.answersmall.lenp90} (answerai-colbert-small).
The models differ in their score margins.
For answerai-colbert-small, the candidates of a query differ little: the median gap between the first and the tenth score is \nm{margin.scifact.answersmall}\% (SciFact) and \nm{margin.nfcorpus.answersmall}\% (NFCorpus) of the first score.
For ColBERTv2 it is \nm{margin.scifact.colbertv2}\% and \nm{margin.nfcorpus.colbertv2}\%.
A possible explanation is that a length error of about 1\% can reorder answerai-colbert-small's candidates more often than ColBERTv2's; the first-to-tenth gap is a coarse measure, so this is not a demonstrated mechanism.
The direction error of a decoded token (cosine about 0.995) affects contracts S and C alike; the length error affects only S, which fits the observation that C removes most of the loss.
Contract C divides by the length of the decoded token; on the same int4 data it gives \nm{nd.scifact.answersmall.Cb} on SciFact and \nm{nd.nfcorpus.answersmall.Cb} on NFCorpus, against float32 values of \nm{nd.scifact.answersmall.F} and \nm{nd.nfcorpus.answersmall.F}.
It removes most, not all, of the loss, and it would still fail the margin of 0.01 on both sets: its own difference to F is \nm{ph.scifact.answersmall.Cb.diff} [\nm{ph.scifact.answersmall.Cb.lo}, \nm{ph.scifact.answersmall.Cb.hi}] and \nm{ph.nfcorpus.answersmall.Cb.diff} [\nm{ph.nfcorpus.answersmall.Cb.lo}, \nm{ph.nfcorpus.answersmall.Cb.hi}].
This analysis is post hoc and descriptive.
We found the loss on the fresh held-out data, so any changed scoring rule needs new data for its test.
The loss belongs to the scoring contract that \sweet implements, not to its floating-point arithmetic.

\paragraph{NumKong's quality.}
NumKong's own scores fell far below the exact value of its own contract with answerai-colbert-small: \nm{nd.scifact.answersmall.NK} against \nm{nd.scifact.answersmall.Ca} on SciFact, below even the BM25 order.
With ColBERTv2 the loss was small (\nm{nd.scifact.colbertv2.NK} against \nm{nd.scifact.colbertv2.Ca}).
On BEIR calls its top-ranked document agreed with the contract-C reference of its own inputs on only \nm{nk.top1own.beir.min}--\nm{nk.top1own.beir.max}\% of calls (agent calls: \nm{nk.top1own.agent-ho.min}--\nm{nk.top1own.agent-ho.max}\%), so the approximation, not the contract, causes most of the disagreement; its bfloat16 path on the Xeon, which uses AMX and returned no NaN, agreed with contract S on 78\%.
A post-hoc check on synthetic vectors explains the collapse (\texttt{numkong\_check.py}).
Our Rust loop and NumKong's own Python interface returned identical scores (largest difference \nm{nkcheck.adapter}), so the adapter is not the cause.
When the candidates' scores lay about \nm{nkcheck.random.gap}\% apart, NumKong's candidate order kept a rank correlation of \nm{nkcheck.random.rho.min}--\nm{nkcheck.random.rho.max} with the exact order.
When they lay about \nm{nkcheck.clustered.gap}\% apart, the correlation fell to \nm{nkcheck.clustered.rho.min}--\nm{nkcheck.clustered.rho.max}, and the top document agreed in \nm{nkcheck.clustered.top1.min}--\nm{nkcheck.clustered.top1.max}\% of queries.
Dimension 96 and 128 behaved the same.
So NumKong's one-token refinement fails when candidate scores lie close together, as they do for answerai-colbert-small, and holds up better when they lie far apart, as for ColBERTv2.
Figure~\ref{fig:nkgap} repeats the check at \nm{nkgap.levels} levels of spread (post hoc, synthetic).
For gaps below 0.5\% the rank correlation stayed at or below \nm{nkgap.low.rho.max}; for random vectors (gap about \nm{nkgap.random.gap}\%) it reached \nm{nkgap.random.rho.min}--\nm{nkgap.random.rho.max}.
The median gaps of answerai-colbert-small (\nm{margin.nfcorpus.answersmall}--\nm{margin.scifact.answersmall}\%) lie on the steep part of the curve; those of ColBERTv2 (\nm{margin.scifact.colbertv2}--\nm{margin.nfcorpus.colbertv2}\%) lie far to its right.

\paragraph{The kernel across CPUs.}
The kernel's BEIR scores were bit-identical across the three x86 CPUs (AVX-512 on Intel and AMD, AVX2 on the EPYC, by design) and between Graviton4 and the M3 Max (both NEON).
Between x86 and ARM, \nm{xplat.armequal.lo}--\nm{xplat.armequal.hi}\% of scores were bit-identical, and the largest difference was \nm{xplat.maxabs}.
The full candidate ranking was the same on at least \nm{xplat.sameranking.min}\% of calls, and nDCG@10 agreed to four decimals.
Within one CPU, the scores of all thread budgets, rounds and processes were bit-identical (\nm{xplat.files} result files, \nm{xplat.scores} million scores).

\subsection{Quality per latency budget}
\label{sec:budget}

A faster scorer can rerank more candidates in the same time.
But \sweet reached a larger $k$ than every exact rival only on AMD, and only at the lower of our two budgets.
Figure~\ref{fig:budget} plots nDCG@10 against the warm replay p95 of the timed scoring call (the 95th percentile over all timed repetitions of the BEIR calls, not a service-request distribution), one point per $k$. Each point's quality comes from the arm's first run; every rival's scores were bit-identical across its runs, except NumKong's Python arms on Intel.
Table~\ref{tab:budget} reads it at two budgets.
The time covers only the scoring call: the rivals' decoding and casting run outside their timer.
The dashed line is a constructed estimate for float32 storage: the reference quality F paired with the time of the fastest frozen exact float32 rival on the same shapes. No single run produced these pairs.
The estimate assumes that the rival's time does not depend on the vector values and that its nDCG@10 would equal F.
A post-freeze check on the M3 Max agrees with both assumptions (Appendix~\ref{app:deviations}).
NumPy and PyTorch scored the original float32 vectors and the int4-decoded vectors of the four $k = \nm{f32d.k}$ sets, in alternating order.
On the original vectors their nDCG@10 was equal to F in every cell.
Their p95 on the original vectors was \nm{f32d.ratio.min}--\nm{f32d.ratio.max} times their p95 on the decoded vectors.
The check ran on one CPU only.
The dotted line is the BM25 order without reranking.

At a p95 budget of 0.25\,ms, \sweet reached a larger $k$ than every exact rival on AMD; at the higher budget, the best exact rival on AMD reached the same $k$.
On Intel the best exact rival (PyTorch with bfloat16 inputs) reached a larger $k$ at both budgets.
On Graviton4, the M3 Max and the EPYC, \sweet and the best exact rival reached the same $k$, or the rival reached a larger one.
For ColBERTv2, more candidates do not always help: on SciFact, nDCG@10 is highest at $k = 25$.
For answerai-colbert-small, the constructed float32-storage estimate lies above every int4 arm at the same budget on every CPU, because of the loss above.
NumKong reached $k = 200$ within 0.25\,ms on the three cloud CPUs.
On Intel its bfloat16 path, which uses AMX, also gave better quality than \sweet with answerai-colbert-small at a lower p95 (Table~\ref{tab:budget}); on the other CPUs NumKong's quality with that model collapsed.
The budget view reads the largest $k$ that fits, which is not always the $k$ with the best quality.

\subsection{Pool-parked calls (5\,ms busy gap)}
\label{sec:parked}

After 5\,ms of other work before each call, NumPy was faster than \sweet on four of the five CPUs.
By then the worker pool of \sweet has parked and must wake (Table~\ref{tab:parked}).
NumPy was faster than \sweet on the EPYC (\nm{parked.epyc.agent-ho.numpy.f32}$\times$ and \nm{parked.epyc.gcsn-ho.numpy.f32}$\times$), on Intel (\nm{parked.intel.agent-ho.numpy.f32}$\times$ on agent calls, \nm{parked.intel.gcsn-ho.numpy.f32}$\times$ on GCSN calls, \nm{parked.intel.beir.numpy.f32}$\times$ on BEIR calls), on Graviton4 (\nm{parked.arm.agent-ho.numpy.f32}$\times$ and \nm{parked.arm.gcsn-ho.numpy.f32}$\times$) and on the M3 Max (\nm{parked.m3max.agent-ho.numpy.f32}$\times$ and \nm{parked.m3max.gcsn-ho.numpy.f32}$\times$).
On AMD, \sweet stayed faster than every exact rival.
The product path calls a prewarm function when the rerank stage starts, so the pool wakes while other work runs.
In this mode the harness runs 0.1\,ms of busy work between the prewarm and the call, and that time is outside the timer.
With it, a call took \nm{range.parked.prewarm.agent.min}--\nm{range.parked.prewarm.agent.max}$\times$ the time of the bare kernel on agent calls.
The result depends on that overlap, and the rivals had no prewarm, so this comparison is not symmetric.
With prewarm, \sweet was still slower than NumPy on Intel GCSN calls (\nm{parked.intel.gcsn-ho.node-prewarm} against \nm{parked.intel.gcsn-ho.numpy.f32}) and on Graviton4 GCSN calls (\nm{parked.arm.gcsn-ho.node-prewarm} against \nm{parked.arm.gcsn-ho.numpy.f32}).
On the EPYC, prewarm did not shorten the call at all (\nm{parked.epyc.agent-ho.node-prewarm} and \nm{parked.epyc.gcsn-ho.node-prewarm} of the bare kernel's time).
So \sweet stayed clearly slower than NumPy (\nm{parked.epyc.agent-ho.numpy.f32} and \nm{parked.epyc.gcsn-ho.numpy.f32}).
On the Mac and the EPYC box the BEIR busy-gap groups were not run (Appendix~\ref{app:deviations}).

\subsection{Smallest-call probe (post-freeze)}
\label{sec:floor}

For the exact rivals, the smallest possible call took \nm{range.floor.exact.min}--\nm{range.floor.exact.max}\% of the time of a median agent call.
At tens of microseconds per call, a rival's time could in principle be mostly fixed cost: Python dispatch, thread wake-up and layout handling.
After the freeze we probed this by timing every arm on two calls, on development data only.
The first is the smallest possible call: one query token and one candidate of one token.
The second is the median development agent call (\nm{floor.shape.nq} query tokens, \nm{floor.shape.pool} candidates, \nm{floor.shape.tokens} candidate tokens).
Each arm ran in three fresh processes, with 200 warm-up calls and five rounds of timed calls (Appendix~\ref{app:deviations}).

For the exact rivals, the smallest call took \nm{range.floor.exact.min}--\nm{range.floor.exact.max}\% of the median call's time (Table~\ref{tab:floor}); for \sweet it took \nm{range.floor.ours.min}--\nm{range.floor.ours.max}\%.
In each of the \nm{floor.beyond.n} CPU--library pairs where \sweet's median call was shorter, the rival's median call minus its smallest call still took at least \nm{floor.beyond.min} times \sweet's whole median call.
This probe does not split a median call into fixed and variable cost.
A larger call can take another code path, allocate more memory or wake more threads.
So its fixed cost can be larger than the smallest call's time.
The probe therefore does not show why \sweet's median call was shorter.

NumKong is different.
Its native loop had the largest fixed share (\nm{range.floor.numkong.min}--\nm{range.floor.numkong.max}\%), and on Intel, Graviton4 and the EPYC its median call was shorter than \sweet's.
On the EPYC this disagrees with the main run, where NumKong needed \nm{warm.epyc.agent-ho.numkong.best}$\times$ the time of \sweet on agent calls (Table~\ref{tab:warm}).
In the main run, the median over held-out agent calls was \nm{p50.epyc.agent.numkong}\,$\mu$s for NumKong's native float32 loop and \nm{p50.epyc.agent.ours}\,$\mu$s for \sweet.
In the fixed-cost run, NumKong's single median call took \nm{floor.epyc.numkong.med}\,$\mu$s and \sweet's \nm{floor.epyc.ours.med}\,$\mu$s.
So NumKong's time halved between the two runs, while \sweet's fell by about 10\%.
A post-freeze check on the EPYC found the cause: the two harnesses repeat calls differently (Appendix~\ref{app:deviations}, item 33).
The main harness times each call after one warm-up call; the probe repeats one call 200 times first.
On \nm{nkw.calls} development agent calls, NumKong took a median of \nm{nkw.nk.1}\,$\mu$s after one warm-up call and \nm{nkw.nk.200}\,$\mu$s after 200.
\sweet took \nm{nkw.ours.min}--\nm{nkw.ours.max}\,$\mu$s at every warm-up count.
NumKong reached its steady speed only after about 50 back-to-back calls (\nm{nkw.nk.50}\,$\mu$s).
So under continuous load, NumKong's native arm was faster than \sweet on these calls.
The main run's per-call protocol includes a start-up cost for this arm that \sweet does not have.
The native arm runs NumKong's kernel in the benchmark's own Rust thread pool, so this cost may come from our adapter, not from NumKong.
We did not separate thread wake-up from clock ramp-up, and we tested only these two arms.

\subsection{Parallel callers (post-freeze rerun)}
\label{sec:conc}

With 16 callers in one shared process, \sweet's throughput fell and an exact rival had the higher throughput in \nm{conc.n16.threads.lost} of \nm{conc.pairs} CPU--workload pairs; with one process per caller, in \nm{conc.n16.procs.lost}.
In a service, several requests can score at the same time.
We ran $N \in \{1, 4, 16\}$ callers in two executors.
The \emph{threads} executor runs $N$ threads in one process, so all callers share the library's pool, as in one service process.
The \emph{procs} executor runs one process per caller with a thread budget of $\max(1, \text{cores}/N)$.
\emph{Closed-loop saturation} lets each caller replay its share of the set back to back; we report throughput.
\emph{Open-loop} runs use one Poisson arrival trace per rate (seed 42; the same timestamps and call order for every arm).
The rates are absolute and common to all arms on a machine; we treat them as fixed stress rates, not as utilization levels (Appendix~\ref{app:conc-frozen}).
Their latency includes queueing.
The threads executor feeds one shared queue; the procs executor assigns each request to one process in advance.

The frozen parallel-caller harness had four defects (Appendix~\ref{app:conc-frozen}).
After the freeze we fixed them in a copy of the harness.
We ran it again with the same arms, sets, rates and seed, under a plan written before the run.
Each configuration ran three times, for at least 3\,s each, and we report the median; Appendix~\ref{app:deviations} (item 27) gives the details.
Over the three runs, the kernel's closed-loop throughput varied by a median of \nm{conc.ours.spread.median}\% (maximum minus minimum, over the median).
On the M3 Max and the EPYC, a separate check compared the scores of parallel calls with those of the same calls alone (\texttt{rerun/conc\_check.py}).
In all \nm{cc.rows} runs, with 1 and 16 callers in both executors, no score differed.

Table~\ref{tab:conc} and Figure~\ref{fig:conc} give the results.
With one caller, \sweet had \nm{range.conc.n1.min}--\nm{range.conc.n1.max} times the throughput of the best exact rival in the threads executor, and \nm{range.conc.n1.procs.min}--\nm{range.conc.n1.procs.max} times in the procs executor.
The two executors now agree at one caller: on every CPU except the M3 Max, \sweet's throughput differed between them by at most \nm{conc.n1.agree.max}\%.
NumKong had a higher throughput than \sweet with one caller in \nm{conc.n1.threads.nklost} of \nm{conc.pairs} CPU--workload pairs (Intel and Graviton4, and EPYC agent calls).
With 16 callers in separate processes, an exact rival had the higher throughput in \nm{conc.n16.procs.lost} of \nm{conc.pairs} pairs, and NumKong in \nm{conc.n16.procs.nklost}.
With 16 callers in one shared process, \sweet's throughput was \nm{conc.thrdrop.min}--\nm{conc.thrdrop.max}\% lower than with one caller, on every CPU.
An exact rival then had the higher throughput in \nm{conc.n16.threads.lost} of \nm{conc.pairs} pairs, and NumKong in \nm{conc.n16.threads.nklost}.
\sweet's worker pool has one thread per performance core and serves every call (Section~\ref{sec:kernel}), so 16 calls in one process share one pool.
For many requests at once, one process per caller was the better setting here.
In the open loop at the highest common rate, the best exact rival had the lower p99 in \nm{conc.open.lost} of \nm{conc.pairs} pairs.
On EPYC GCSN calls the two p99 values differ by less than 1\% (\nm{conc.epyc.gcsn.open.ours} against \nm{conc.epyc.gcsn.open.exact}\,ms).
On M3 Max GCSN calls, the best exact rival had the lower p99 (\nm{conc.m3max.gcsn.open.exact} against \nm{conc.m3max.gcsn.open.ours}\,ms).

The M3 Max values come from a recheck, because \sweet's throughput in the first rerun fell from each of its three runs to the next.
The recheck ran each configuration in its own process, in three passes with different orders (Appendix~\ref{app:deviations}, item 32).
Over the three passes, the throughput of a configuration varied by a median of \nm{m3c.rest.spread.median}\%.
One \sweet agent pass was an outlier, with \nm{m3c.first.ours} against \nm{m3c.other.ours.lo}--\nm{m3c.other.ours.hi} thousand calls/s at one caller, and NumPy varied by up to \nm{m3c.numpy.spread.max}\%.
Table~\ref{tab:conc} marks these rows, and its values are the medians of the three passes.

\subsection{Memory}
\label{sec:memory}

The int4 store makes \sweet the smallest arm in memory: \nm{mem.intel.ours.bpt} bytes per stored token on Intel, against \nm{mem.intel.numpy_packed.bpt} for NumPy.
A separate, untimed pass per arm and set measured resident memory in a fresh process.
On Linux it reads \texttt{VmRSS}, \texttt{VmHWM} and \texttt{smaps\_rollup}; on macOS a 0.2\,ms sampler reads the resident size.
The allocator returned freed large blocks at once, so a later phase cannot reuse them unseen.
Table~\ref{tab:memory} reports resident bytes per stored document token and the peak temporary memory of the largest call.
The first number adds the computed size of the int4 store (for arms that read it) to the measured resident size of the arm's own prepared copy.
The memory pass uses other allocator settings than the timing runs, and the Mac's 0.2\,ms sampler can miss short peaks, so Mac peaks are lower bounds.
\sweet holds \nm{mem.intel.ours.bpt} bytes per token at dimension 128 and \nm{mem.intel.ours.bpt96} at dimension 96, the size of the int4 store itself; this follows from the format.
The float32 rivals hold their decoded copy: about 520 bytes per token for NumPy and \nm{mem.intel.torch_pad_bmm.bpt} for padded PyTorch on Intel.
NumKong's packed format holds more than its input precision: \nm{mem.amd.numkong_native_f32.bpt} bytes per token for float32 and \nm{mem.amd.numkong_native_bf16.bpt} for bfloat16 on AMD.
This fits its method: the int8 screen needs an int8 copy of every token (128 bytes at dimension 128) beside the full-precision copy.
The remainder of 35 to 150 bytes per token depends on the CPU.
The int4-self rivals hold the store and peak at about 280\,MB of temporary memory on the largest agent call.

\subsection{Instance-to-instance spread}
\label{sec:spread}

A replica instance of each cloud type repeated the agent and GCSN sets.
Across \nm{replica.n} rival ratios, the ratio on the replica differed from the first instance by a median of \nm{replica.median}\% and at most \nm{replica.maxabs}\%.
No ratio changed sides of 1 (Table~\ref{tab:replica}).
The kernel's own geometric-mean latency differed by at most \nm{replica.oursmax}\%.
Each replica was a different instance in the same availability zone (Section~\ref{sec:limits} discusses the hosts).
After the freeze, two new instances of each cloud type repeated the BEIR sets: one ran the sets with $k = 100$, the other the sets with $k = 25$.
This rerun was planned before it ran (Appendix~\ref{app:deviations}).
Across \nm{rbeir.n} frozen rival configurations, the ratio on the new instances differed by a median of \nm{rbeir.median}\% and at most \nm{rbeir.max}\%.
Of these ratios, \nm{rbeir.flips} changed sides of 1.
The kernel's own geometric-mean latency differed by at most \nm{rbeir.oursmax}\%, and its scores were bit-identical to the first run.
The sweep sets have no replica, and the M3 Max and the EPYC are single machines.
Differences smaller than about 13\% there, such as M3 Max BEIR NumKong (0.99), may not repeat on another machine.
