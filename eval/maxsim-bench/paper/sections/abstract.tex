\begin{abstract}
\noindent
Libraries that compute late-interaction (MaxSim) scores for ColBERT-style reranking do not all compute the same score: they differ in normalization, epsilon placement, clamping and padding.
We present \benchname, a benchmark for CPU MaxSim kernels that defines each scoring rule as a contract, computes a float64 reference per contract, and checks every implementation against the reference of its own rule.
These checks found two new defects in other libraries, among them invalid scores from NumKong on Intel CPUs with AMX, and confirmed a known one.
The benchmark replays rerank calls captured from a code-search engine and from two BEIR datasets, and keeps development and held-out data apart under a recorded freeze.
We use it to compare an int4 SIMD kernel from the open-source engine sweet-search with NumPy, PyTorch, maxsim-cpu, NextPlaid and NumKong on five CPUs.
With warm calls, the kernel was faster than every exact float32 rival on the captured agent and code-search calls on all five CPUs; on agent calls the fastest rival needed \nm{best.warm.agent-ho.f32.min}--\nm{best.warm.agent-ho.f32.max}$\times$ its time.
These sets were examined during development; on the fresh BEIR calls the kernel was faster on four CPUs, and PyTorch was faster on the Xeon.
Rivals were faster in other conditions, which we list: one thread, many callers in one process, a busy gap before the call, large calls, and NumKong's approximate scores.
A pre-registered test showed that the kernel's int4 scoring rule keeps nDCG@10 for ColBERTv2 but loses up to \nm{q.intel.nfcorpus.answersmall.relabs}\% for answerai-colbert-small.

\end{abstract}
