\section{Results}\label{sec:results}

All numbers in this section are held-out: \nm{B-records} frozen points of comparison (B) and
\nm{A-records} points of comparison (A), over five CPUs, seven sets and three models.

\subsection{Comparison (A): generators under a common rescoring procedure}

Table~\ref{tab:compA-intel} and Figure~\ref{fig:compA} show every generator at the budget
$C = 400$, $H = 100$ and its smaller declared effort. Under the same int8 screen and the same
float16 rescoring, retained quality depends on the generator only through its 400 candidates. Every
graph generator and every flat scan keeps at least 98.4\% of exact quality on every set and CPU (FAISS
binary HNSW 98.7\%, USearch b1 98.4\% at their lowest; our walk 99.3\%, our scan 99.6\%). The two
partition generators do not at their declared effort: with the smallest \texttt{nprobe} or leaf count
that returns 400 candidates, FAISS RaBitQ-IVF keeps 57--92\% and ScaNN 23--100\%, so their speed on the
large sets comes with a large quality loss. Across all CPUs, \nm{A-lowq} rival generator--set cells stay below 0.99:
\nm{A-lowq-part} of the partition generators and \nm{A-lowq-graph} of the binary graphs, which stay
between 0.984 and 0.99. Their larger effort is in the full grid.

Among the generators that keep at least 99\% of exact quality, one of our two generators (exact
binary scan or binary HNSW walk) is the fastest in all \nm{A-oursbest} of \nm{A-n} (CPU, set) cells.
The next fastest such generator is \nm{A-min} to \nm{A-max} times slower (geometric mean \nm{A-gm}).
Our exact scan is the faster of our two generators up to 22k vectors on every CPU (the walk takes
1.2--1.6 times as long). On FiQA and dall the walk is faster on the x86 CPUs and the Mac (0.6--0.9 of
the scan's time) and slower on Graviton (1.4 times); above 150k vectors only the walk runs. The flat scans of the rivals (FAISS binary flat, RaBitQ flat) grow linearly
with corpus size, the float32 graphs (hnswlib, FAISS HNSW) pay for float distance computations, and
the binary graphs (FAISS binary HNSW, USearch b1) take 1.9--7.3 times as long as our walk at the same
$ef$ and budget.
We did not profile the rival generators; the comparison shows the cost of each library's own
candidate step, not why it costs what it does.

\subsection{Comparison (B): as shipped, at the same retained quality}

Table~\ref{tab:headline099} compares our cascade with the fastest rival that keeps 99\% of exact
quality on held-out queries; Figure~\ref{fig:size} plots the same points against corpus size. The
answer depends on corpus size more than on the CPU.

\begin{itemize}\setlength{\itemsep}{1pt}
\item \textbf{Large sets} (Quora, 523k; csn1m, 1M): ours is faster in \nm{faster-large} of
\nm{n-large} cells (whole interval above 1), slower in \nm{slower-large}; ratios \nm{min-large} to
\nm{max-large}, geometric mean \nm{gm-large}. The fastest rivals here are graph indexes (FAISS HNSW
with SQ8 or binary codes, SVS Vamana with LVQ or LeanVec on Intel, USearch b1).
\item \textbf{Middle sets} (FiQA, 58k; dall, 140k): mixed. Ours is faster in \nm{faster-mid} of
\nm{n-mid} cells and slower in \nm{slower-mid} (geometric mean \nm{gm-mid}). FiQA with jina is our
largest margin outside the large sets: the rivals need deep refine pools to keep 99\% there.
\item \textbf{Small code sets} (gcsn, d20k, AdvTest; 7k--22k): the rivals win more often. Ours is
slower in \nm{slower-small} of \nm{n-small} cells and faster in \nm{faster-small} (geometric mean
\nm{gm-small}). The winner is usually FAISS binary flat: an exact Hamming scan of the same 512-bit
codes, followed by float refine, in C++.
\end{itemize}

Over all CPUs and sets, the geometric mean of the ratio at $\fr = 0.99$ is \nm{gm-all-099}
(\nm{gmn-all-099} cells, ours faster in \nm{wins-all-099}); per CPU it is \nm{gm-intel-099} (Intel),
\nm{gm-amd-099} (AMD), \nm{gm-arm-099} (Graviton), \nm{gm-box-099} (box, reduced grid) and
\nm{gm-mac-099} (Mac, reduced grid). At $\fr = 0.98$ the rivals gain (\nm{gm-all-098}); at
$\fr = 0.995$ ours gains (\nm{gm-all-0995}), but fewer cells have points at that fraction
(Appendix~\ref{app:fractions}). The CPU changes the size of the margins more than their sign; the
large sets are mostly wins and the small code sets mostly losses on every CPU, while some middle cells
change sign between CPUs (FiQA with bge: 0.88 on Intel, 0.69 on AMD, 1.41 on Graviton).

\paragraph{Held-out confirmation of the dev points.} Every frozen point was chosen on dev to meet its
fraction with the one-sided 97.5\% bound. On held-out queries most points still meet it by the point
estimate, but many no longer meet it by the bound (the tables mark both). This is expected: a point
chosen as the \emph{fastest} that passes on dev sits close to the boundary, and a fresh sample moves it
both ways. It holds for ours and for the rivals alike; we report it rather than re-select.

\subsection{Primary comparisons}

Table~\ref{tab:primary} gives the \nm{nprimary} confirmatory comparisons. Quality non-inferiority
at $\delta = \nm{delta}$ passes in \nm{nprimarypass} of \nm{nprimary} after Holm. The three failures
are FiQA with bge-base-en-v1.5 on all three CPUs, where the held-out set is small (259 queries) and
the power was lowest (Appendix~\ref{app:power}); the observed differences there are $-0.0015$ to
$+0.0005$. On latency, ours is faster in \nm{nprimaryfaster} pairs and slower in
\nm{nprimaryslower} (whole interval on one side of 1). The pairs where ours is slower are SVS on
Intel (FiQA and Quora), FAISS IVF-PQ fast scan and hnswlib on AMD FiQA, and FAISS binary HNSW on
Graviton Quora with bge.

\subsection{Memory, load and build}

Table~\ref{tab:mem-csn1m} shows memory and cold start at 1M vectors (Quora and dall in
Appendix~\ref{app:mem}). Our cascade holds the graph, the codes, the int8 rows and the float16
vectors, memory-mapped from its index files, plus the Node.js runtime: \nm{mem-ours-intel}~GB on
Intel (\nm{mem-ours-amd} on AMD, \nm{mem-ours-arm} on Graviton). That is more than
\nm{mem-smaller-intel} of the \nm{mem-nriv-intel} rival points on Intel; the smallest is USearch int8
at \nm{mem-rivalmin-intel}~GB, and the rivals that refine with float32 originals sit at 3.0--3.7~GB (FAISS HNSW-SQ8 at \nm{mem-rivalmax-intel}~GB).
Our cold start (load plus first query on a dropped page cache) is \nm{cold-ours-intel}~s on Intel
against \nm{cold-rivalmin-intel}--\nm{cold-rivalmax-intel}~s for the rivals; as noted in
\S\ref{sec:systems}, the rival numbers include the harness's buffered read of the originals, so we
treat this comparison as indicative. Table~\ref{tab:build} lists build cost at 1M: our build took
\nm{build-ours-s}~s (\nm{build-ours-h}~hours) on one thread with a peak of \nm{build-ours-peak}~GB,
the longest wall time of any build (the longest rival build took \nm{build-rival-max-s}~s on 16
threads).
