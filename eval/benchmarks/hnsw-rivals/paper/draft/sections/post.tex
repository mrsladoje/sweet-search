\section{Post-freeze experiments}\label{sec:post}

The experiments in this section ran after the held-out run, on held-out queries, with the frozen builds
and operating points and with harness changes that only add clocks and switches (Appendix~\ref{app:deviations}).
They answer two questions that the main results leave open; they are not part of the pre-registered study.

\subsection{Generator time alone}

We added per-query clocks around the stages of comparison (A) in both harnesses: query conversion, the
generator, and the shared screen plus rescoring. We reran (A) at $C = 400$, $H = 100$ on the three AWS CPUs
(one model per set kind, 5 rounds), with every declared effort, with the four graph families also at the
build our graph uses ($M = 64$, $ef_{\text{construction}} = 800$), and with our walk also at a fixed $ef$,
without early termination and without huge pages.

\paragraph{Shared stages.} The screen plus rescoring took a median \nm{post-shared-ours-intel}~\us{} in our
implementation and \nm{post-shared-riv-intel}~\us{} in the rivals' C++ path on Intel
(\nm{post-shared-x-intel}$\times$), \nm{post-shared-x-amd}$\times$ on AMD and \nm{post-shared-x-arm}$\times$ on
Graviton: the gap estimated in \S\ref{sec:results} is real and of that size.

\paragraph{Generators at equal settings.} With the same graph build ($M = 64$), the same $ef$, our walk
without its adaptive rule, early termination or huge pages, and the generator clock alone, the rival
graph generators took longer than our walk in all \nm{post-eq-n} comparisons. The comparison that isolates the
implementation is the one on the same codes: FAISS binary HNSW and USearch b1 walk graphs over the same 512-bit sign
codes with Hamming distance, and took \nm{post-eqb-min} to \nm{post-eqb-max} times as long as our walk (geometric mean
\nm{post-eqb-gm}, \nm{post-eqb-n} comparisons). hnswlib and FAISS HNSW walk the same kind of graph with 768-dimensional
float32 distances and took \nm{post-eqf-min} to \nm{post-eqf-max} times as long (geometric mean \nm{post-eqf-gm}); that
gap is mostly the cost of the distance function, not of the walk, and it buys candidates of higher quality at small
budgets (\S\ref{sec:results}).

\paragraph{Why the binary walk is faster.} Reading the sources shows two differences in the data structures. Our
walk keeps its candidate and result queues as one bucket per integer Hamming distance (0 to 512), so a push is $O(1)$
and a pop amortised $O(1)$; FAISS and USearch keep binary heaps. Our exact scan selects the best $C$ from a histogram of
the 513 possible distances, while FAISS binary flat keeps a binary heap of size $C$ by default (a counting variant
exists, \texttt{use\_heap = false}). After the reviews of this draft we measured both on one machine, the Mac
(held-out, $C = 400$, generator clock, 5 rounds, every set with one model per set kind; Table~\ref{tab:mech}). With
binary heaps instead of buckets our walk takes \nm{mech-hb-fixed} times as long at fixed $ef$ (\nm{mech-hb-fixed-min}--\nm{mech-hb-fixed-max}
over sets) and \nm{mech-hb-adapt} times with its adaptive rule. FAISS binary HNSW at the same build and $ef$ takes
\nm{mech-fb} times as long as our walk with buckets and still \nm{mech-fh} times as long as our walk with heaps: the queues
explain about \nm{mech-share}\% of the gap on a log scale, and the rest, which we did not locate, lies elsewhere (visited-set
handling, neighbour-list layout and per-step overheads are candidates). For the flat scan, FAISS's counting top-$k$ is
\nm{mech-flat-hc} times faster than its default heap, and our scan is still \nm{mech-flat-co} times faster than the counting
variant. So the selection structure explains part of both gaps, and an implementation that keeps the default heap
understates what FAISS binary flat can do; comparison (B) used the default.

\subsection{Slow rounds and the large sets in comparison (B)}

Every record of the post-freeze runs counts the major page faults of its timed pass. Our cascade had
\nm{post-slow-ours} slow rounds in \nm{post-rounds-ours}, the rivals \nm{post-slow-riv} in
\nm{post-rounds-riv}. Every slow round of ours had at least \nm{post-slow-faults-min} major page faults in its timed pass
(median \nm{post-slow-faults-med}), against a median of \nm{post-fast-faults-med} in our other rounds. So
in a slow round, pages of our memory-mapped files were read from disk during the timed pass, although
the pre-read before the job had loaded them: they had been evicted in between. In comparison (A) the stage
clocks place the extra time of 12 of the 16 slow rounds in the full-vector rescoring (the float16 vector
file) and of the other 4 in the int8 screen (the int8 rows); the generator is never the slow stage. A
server whose index shares the page cache with other I/O can see the same effect; the rivals, which read
their indexes into anonymous memory, cannot.

On Quora and csn1m we reran our frozen point and the rival fastest on dev at $\fr = 0.99$, with the CPU's
$R$ rounds (Table~\ref{tab:postb}). The geometric mean of the ratio over the \nm{postb-n} cells is
\nm{postb-gm} (ours faster by point estimate in \nm{postb-wins}). The native calls of our cascade take
\nm{postb-native-min}--\nm{postb-native-max}\% of its timed query (median \nm{postb-native-med}\%); the rest
is JavaScript orchestration and metadata reads. Our shipped default ($S_1 = 400$) takes
\nm{postb-default-tmin}--\nm{postb-default-tmax} times the time of our frozen point: more where the frozen point
uses a smaller $S_1$ (below the product floor), less on csn1m with bge, where the frozen point uses
$S_1 = 2000$ or 3000 and the default retains only \nm{postb-default-qmin} of exact quality, below
$\fr = 0.99$.

\subsection{Which binary code?}\label{sec:codeablation}

Our codes take the sign of the first 512 of 768 dimensions, and none of the three models reports training for nested
dimensions (\S\ref{sec:related}). After the reviews of this draft we checked three alternatives, for quality only: the
sign of all 768 dimensions, and the sign of the first 512 or all 768 dimensions after a random orthogonal rotation
(seed 42). Stage 1 is an exact Hamming scan of the code (top $C$, ties to the lower vector id); every candidate is then
rescored with the float32 cosine, so the code is the only difference. Table~\ref{tab:codeablation} counts the
\nm{ca-cells} set--model cells whose held-out retained quality meets $\fr = 0.99$ by the selection rule's bound. With
768-bit codes, \nm{ca-sign768-100} cells meet it at $C = 100$, against \nm{ca-sign512-100} with our 512-bit codes, and
\nm{ca-sign768-400} against \nm{ca-sign512-400} at $C = 400$; the gain is largest for bge-base-en-v1.5 on the code sets and
for FiQA. The tested random rotation before the 512-bit cut does not recover that benefit (\nm{ca-rot512-100} cells at
$C = 100$); keeping all dimensions does. One rotation does not rule out a learned one. A 768-bit code costs 50\% more code memory and Hamming work per
candidate; we did not time it. These results support the small-budget losses of comparison (A) (\S\ref{sec:losses}) and
point to the first change we would test in the product.
